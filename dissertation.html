Dissertation

Self-Control from Childhood to Adolescence

Development, Family Origins, and Criminal Justice Involvement into Early Adulthood

Three empirical tests of what the general theory of crime claims about self-control: that it is a single trait, that parenting forms it in early childhood, and that it shapes official records only through delinquency.

All three papers use the UK Millennium Cohort Study, with parent ratings at ages 5 to 17 and self-reports through age 23. Methods include longitudinal measurement invariance, latent growth, cross-lagged panel, and discrete-time survival models.

Paper one · Developmental course Drafted

Two Domains, Two Developmental Courses

“Dimensions of Self-Control: Hyperactivity/Inattention and Conduct Problems from Ages 5 to 17.”

Main finding

The two behavioral domains routinely combined as one self-control trait do not develop together. Hyperactivity/inattention declines about three times faster after age 11 than before it, while conduct problems decline slowly and at a nearly constant rate. Self-control keeps changing after age 10, and the two domains should be modeled separately.

The question

Gottfredson and Hirschi treat self-control as a single trait, established by about ages 8 to 10. Tests of the theory have asked whether low self-control predicts delinquency and whether it stops changing after age 10, but rarely whether it is one trait. If restlessness and inattention reflect the same trait as fighting, stealing, and lying, the two sets of behaviors should change at the same rate. Do they?

Why it matters

Behavioral measures of self-control usually sum hyperactivity/inattention and conduct problems into one score. If the two domains change at different rates, that score averages two developmental trajectories and describes neither well, and what it measures shifts with age. Conduct problems items also include delinquent acts, which raises the tautology problem when the score is used to predict delinquency.

What I did

I analyzed parent-rated Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire items from the UK Millennium Cohort Study at ages 5, 7, 11, 14, and 17. After testing longitudinal measurement invariance with the items treated as ordered categories, I estimated a bivariate piecewise latent growth model, with a knot at age 11, for the 5,659 cohort members rated at every age.

What I found

Between ages 5 and 17, hyperactivity/inattention declined by 0.32 standard deviations, 74% of it after age 11. Conduct problems declined by 0.15 standard deviations at a nearly constant rate. The two rates of decline did not differ before age 11 and diverged after it: in adolescence, hyperactivity/inattention declined more than four times as fast as conduct problems on the item metric. The combined score kept declining after age 10, and most of that adolescent decline reflects hyperactivity/inattention. The pattern holds across robustness checks, and teachers’ independent ratings at ages 7 and 11 also show the childhood decline.

What it means

Hyperactivity/inattention and conduct problems are not interchangeable indicators of one trait, at least with respect to development. Behaviorally measured self-control continues to change after age 10, contrary to the stability thesis, and developmental research should estimate the two domains separately. The other two papers build on this by following both domains as well as the combined score.

Paper two · Family origins Drafted

Early Differences Persist; Closeness Responds to the Child

“Family Origins of Self-Control: Early Parenting, Household Disadvantage, and Self-Control Problems from Ages 5 to 17.”

Main finding

Household disadvantage in infancy and harsh, less close, and less structured parenting predict more self-control problems at age 5. By age 17 the parenting gaps are about half as large, while the disadvantage gap does not narrow. Within families, self-control problems precede lower parental closeness, not the reverse.

The question

The general theory of crime makes three claims about where self-control comes from: early parenting sets its level, the differences set early persist, and parenting shapes the child rather than the reverse. These claims have mostly been tested in separate studies. Do they hold when tested together, in the same children, from age 5 to 17?

Why it matters

If early differences persist, the conditions of infancy and early childhood matter well into adolescence. If the parent–child relationship responds to the child rather than shaping the child, then associations between parenting and self-control cannot be read as parenting effects, and the child has to be treated as an active party in the relationship.

What I did

Using parent ratings of 4,743 cohort members at ages 5, 7, 11, 14, and 17, I estimated latent growth models of self-control problems on four early family conditions: household disadvantage at 9 months (low income, low parental qualifications, lone parenthood), harsh discipline, household structure and routine, and parental closeness, net of maternal psychological distress and infant temperament. I then used random-intercept cross-lagged panel models to separate within-person change from stable differences between families and ask which way the relationship between closeness and self-control runs.

What I found

All four family conditions predict more self-control problems at age 5, in both hyperactivity/inattention and conduct problems. The differences associated with harsh discipline, household structure and routine, and parental closeness were still present at age 17 but about half as large as at age 5. The difference associated with household disadvantage did not narrow. Within cohort members, self-control problems above a child’s own average were followed by below-average parental closeness two to four years later, whereas above-average closeness was not followed by fewer self-control problems.

What it means

Consistent with the general theory of crime, differences associated with household disadvantage and early parenting persist into late adolescence. Contrary to it, parental closeness appears to respond to a child’s self-control problems rather than shape them. Household structure and routine is a promising, less studied target for early intervention.

Paper three · Justice-system entry Job market paper Drafted

Differential Selection by Self-Control

“Differential Selection by Self-Control: Self-Control Problems and Entry into the Criminal Justice System from Adolescence to Early Adulthood.”

Main finding

Young people with more self-control problems are more likely to enter the criminal justice system, net of the delinquency they report and the police stops they have experienced. Among youth already in contact with the police, self-control problems, not delinquency, predict formal processing.

The question

Some of the most persuasive evidence for the general theory of crime comes from official records, such as the link between childhood self-control and adult conviction. But an official record documents a decision by the police or the courts as well as what the young person did. Does low self-control lead to official records only because it leads to more delinquency (differential involvement), or also because it shapes how the police and courts respond (differential selection)?

Why it matters

If self-control shapes processing as well as offending, an association between low self-control and an official record combines delinquency with criminal justice decisions. Estimates of the effect of a first sanction on later delinquency also combine the sanction with the self-control of the young people who receive it, which matters for labeling research and for youth justice.

What I did

I followed 5,352 Millennium Cohort Study members from age 14 to age 23 (8,925 person-periods) and estimated discrete-time survival models of a first police caution, arrest, or court appearance. Parent-rated self-control problems predict entry, controlling for self-reported delinquency and earlier police stops. I then looked at formal processing among those in police contact, at hyperactivity/inattention alone, and at within-person increases in self-control problems during adolescence.

What I found

  1. Net of delinquency and prior police stops, self-control problems predict entry into the justice system. Between ages 14 and 17, predicted risk of entry was 6.7% at the 90th percentile of self-control problems and 3.3% at the 10th.
  2. Among young people in contact with the police, self-control problems predict formal processing, while delinquency is not significantly associated with it.
  3. Restlessness and inattention, which involve no delinquent act, also predict entry, so the result is not an artifact of measuring self-control with delinquent behavior. Increases in self-control problems during adolescence predict entry too.

What it means

Low self-control affects how the police and the courts respond to young people, not only how much delinquency they commit, and official records of criminal justice involvement reflect both. The finding that official records and self-reports identify the same people as offenders does not extend to the trait at the center of the general theory of crime.

How the papers connect

Each paper tests one claim the general theory of crime makes about self-control itself, not only about its link to delinquency.

Paper 1 tests whether self-control is a single trait and finds two domains with different developmental courses that keep changing after age 10. Paper 2 tests where self-control comes from and finds that early differences persist, but that parental closeness responds to the child rather than shaping the child. Paper 3 tests whether self-control reaches official records only through delinquency and finds that it also shapes who the police and courts select.
